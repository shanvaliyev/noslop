# Formats

Sections: social posts, long-form, marketing copy and email. Read the one that matches the piece.

## Social posts: X, LinkedIn, Threads, Bluesky

The reader is scrolling, so the first line has about a second to earn attention. Hooks, line breaks, punchy closers and questions to readers work there. Don't strip them out and hand back a polished paragraph; that reads like a press release. A post reads as slop when the mechanics wrap nothing, or when the wording is a line readers have seen hundreds of times.

**Keep the craft, and put something real in it:**

- **Hooks** work best with a specific in them. "Most startup advice is wrong" is generic. "'Talk to 100 customers first' cost me three months" works. A contrast hook is fine unless both halves are vague.
- **Short lines and line breaks** are normal. Use them for pace. The tell is every line the same length.
- **A punchy last line** should add a consequence, a callback or a twist, not restate the post as an aphorism.
- **Questions to readers** work when people can actually answer them. "What did you charge your first client?" beats "Thoughts?"
- **Hype** is fine when the news is real. A launch earns it; a minor update doesn't.
- **Threads and CTAs:** numbering is fine if each post holds a real point. Use "comment X and I'll send it" at most once, and only if the user wants it.

**Still slop on social:** a hook the post never backs up; recycled lines ("Let that sink in," "Read that again," "X is dead"); invented stories or numbers; "5 lessons" listicles where each lesson is generic; emoji at the start of every line; fake-bold Unicode, which screen readers can't read; more than three hashtags; a redemption arc forced onto every failure.

**Platforms:** On X the first line is the whole pitch, so cut for length. On LinkedIn about two lines show before "see more," so the hook has to finish inside them. Threads and Bluesky are more conversational, and heavy LinkedIn hooks read as out of place there.

### Example: keep the structure, add substance

**Before:**

> AI won't replace developers.
>
> But developers who use AI will replace developers who don't.
>
> Read that again. 🔥

**After (the writer had this example):**

> AI didn't replace me this week.
>
> It wrote 2,000 lines of our Postgres migration in an afternoon.
>
> I spent the next two days reading every one of them.

The hook and line breaks stay. The recycled slogan and "Read that again" go, and the post ends on the specific detail. If the writer has no example and wants the original line, that's their call; point out that the line is widely recycled.

## Long-form: blog posts, newsletters, essays

Long-form gives AI habits room to compound. Twelve sections in the same shape, each ending on a lesson, add up to a piece the reader finishes knowing nothing new. Spend most of the effort on structure and specifics.

- **The title makes a claim or names a thing.** "We cut our AWS bill 60% by deleting one service" beats "Optimizing Cloud Costs: A Comprehensive Guide."
- **Open with the most interesting specific:** a number, a scene, a mistake, a claim someone would argue with. Context can come after. If the first paragraph is scene-setting ("In today's rapidly evolving world..."), delete it and see if the second works as an opener.
- **Structure follows the material.** Give the hard part most of the words. Headings say what's under them ("Why the cache made it worse," not "The Challenge"), in sentence case. Short pieces often need no headings.
- **Prose for arguments, bullets for parallel things** like steps or options.
- **Include the mess:** what didn't work, what you still don't know. A model can't generate that.

**Tells:** a roadmap intro ("In this post we'll explore X, Y and Z"); "7 ways" when there are three good ones; a bold "Key takeaway" after every section; "Now that we've covered X, let's turn to Y"; a stock "challenges" section; an "it depends on your needs" conclusion; an inspirational send-off; newsletter boilerplate like "Hope you're having a great week!"

### Example: newsletter section

**Before:**

> ## 📚 What I'm Reading
>
> This week I dove into "The Mom Test" by Rob Fitzpatrick, and wow — what a game-changer! This book is a must-read for any founder looking to master the art of customer conversations. Highly recommend!

**Source notes:** The chapter on compliments being useless data changed how the writer ran interviews. Before, they counted "I'd totally use that" as validation.

**After:**

> ## What I'm reading
>
> *The Mom Test* by Rob Fitzpatrick. The chapter that got me is the one about compliments. I'd been counting "oh, I'd totally use that" as a yes in customer interviews. Fitzpatrick's point is that compliments cost the person nothing, so they tell you nothing. Now I ask what they use today and what it costs them. The conversation is more awkward, and I learn more from it.

## Marketing copy: landing pages, product pages, announcements

Readers come to marketing copy already skeptical. A number, a price, a mechanism or a named customer gives them a reason to keep reading.

- **The headline says what the product does** or what changes for the user. "Send payroll in 10 minutes" beats "The Future of Finance."
- **Prove one claim.** "Setup takes 4 minutes" does more than "seamless, intuitive, and effortless."
- **Explain the mechanism.** Readers trust copy that shows how it works.
- **Write for one kind of buyer.** "For clinics with two to ten chairs" beats "Whether you're a solo practitioner or a large enterprise."
- **Show the price,** or say plainly why you don't.

**Tells:** "Discover the power of," "Unlock your potential," "Imagine a world where"; "Simple. Powerful. Affordable."; solutions, platform, ecosystem; best-in-class and other superlatives without a comparison; fake urgency; "Email, reimagined"; emoji feature grids; "We're thrilled to introduce" before saying what the user gets.

### Example: feature section

**Before:**

> **⚡ Lightning-Fast Performance** — Experience blazing speed that keeps up with your ambitions.
>
> **🔒 Enterprise-Grade Security** — Rest easy knowing your data is protected by industry-leading security.

**Source notes:** Search returns in under 100 ms on 1M documents. SOC 2 Type II certified, AES-256 encryption at rest.

**After:**

> **Search that keeps up.** Results in under 100 ms, even across a million documents.
>
> **SOC 2 Type II.** Data is encrypted at rest with AES-256. The report is available on request.

Bold labels are fine on a landing page when each label is short and the sentence after it adds facts.

## Email

- **Put the ask or the news in the first two lines.** Many readers only see the preview.
- **The subject line says what's inside.** "Contract for Thursday's shoot" beats "Quick question."
- **Make one ask,** and number them if there are two.
- **Write it the way you'd say it across a desk,** and sign off with your name.
- **Follow-ups add something:** a new detail, a deadline or a smaller ask.

**Tells:** "I hope this email finds you well"; "I wanted to reach out"; "I came across your profile and was truly impressed"; "Just circling back"; "Would love to explore potential synergies"; "Please don't hesitate to reach out"; "We're thrilled to share some exciting updates."

### Example: follow-up email

**Before:**

> Hi Marcus,
>
> Just circling back on my previous email! I know you're busy, but I wanted to bump this to the top of your inbox. Would love to find some time to connect and explore how we might work together.

**After:**

> Hi Marcus,
>
> One more thing since I last wrote: we just shipped a Shopify app, so your merchants could add our returns flow without a developer. If that changes the picture, I can send a two-minute video instead of a call.
>
> Jen

This only works if the Shopify app is real. A follow-up with nothing new should be one line ("Still interested? If not, no worries.") or not sent at all.
