# Pattern catalog

Each entry has what to watch for, why it reads as machine-made, and a before/after pair. Tier numbers match SKILL.md: 1 = fix on sight, 2 = fix when it repeats or clusters, 3 = weak alone.

Every entry asks whether a sentence gives the reader something new. If it doesn't, it's a candidate for cutting.

These tiers apply as written to websites, email, docs and articles. Social posts get some slack on hooks, fragments, closers and reader questions; see "Venue changes the rules" in SKILL.md and [social.md](social.md).

Many "After" examples use specific details (names, numbers, dates) to show what the fixed sentence should look like. In real work those specifics have to come from the user or the source. If you don't have them, ask, or write the plainer sentence that doesn't need them. Never invent a detail to make a rewrite land.

## A. Staging instead of stating

These are the strongest tells in current model prose.

### 1. Not X but Y (Tier 1)

**Watch for:** it's not X, it's Y; not just X but Y; not only... but also; X isn't the problem, Y is; the question isn't X, it's Y; the answer isn't X; it feels like X, it's actually Y; stops being X and starts being Y; more than just a; the contrast split across two sentences ("This doesn't mean X. It means Y."); a clipped negative tail ("..., no guesswork," "..., zero fluff").

**Why:** The negative half rebuts something nobody claimed, so the positive half sounds bigger. It makes the claim sound bigger and adds no information.

**Keep it when** the reader really believes X, or both halves carry facts.

> **Before:** Great onboarding isn't about features. It's about momentum.
>
> **After:** Get new users to their first finished report inside ten minutes, and schedule the rest of onboarding after that.

> **Before:** This isn't just a coffee shop. It's a community.
>
> **After:** The coffee shop runs a Thursday chess night, and the same eight people have shown up for two years.

> **Before (split):** Remote work doesn't mean less collaboration. It means more intentional collaboration.
>
> **After:** Our remote team schedules fewer meetings, and each one has a written agenda.

> **Before (clipped tail):** Prices sync automatically from Shopify, no spreadsheets.
>
> **After:** Prices sync from Shopify every 15 minutes, so you can stop updating them by hand.

> **Keep (real correction):** Everyone assumed the outage was the database. It wasn't. A DNS record had expired.

### 2. Staged openers and throat-clearing (Tier 1)

**Watch for:** Here's the thing; Here's what/why/how...; Let's dive in; Let's break it down; Let me be clear; The truth is; The reality is; The uncomfortable truth; It turns out; I'll be honest; Real talk; Honestly?; Look,; So, (opening a paragraph); Can we talk about; Buckle up; Without further ado; Quick note; Heads up.

**Why:** The writer announces the point, or stages a moment of candor, instead of making the point.

**Keep** "honestly" or "look" inside an ordinary casual sentence. The tell is the standalone opener before a routine claim.

> **Before:** Here's the thing about pricing: most founders charge too little. Let me explain.
>
> **After:** Most founders underprice. I charged $19 a month for two years before a customer told me she'd have paid $60.

> **Before:** Is it worth upgrading? Honestly? It depends.
>
> **After:** Upgrade if you export more than a few times a week. Otherwise the free plan covers you.

### 3. Faux-insight setups (Tier 1)

**Watch for:** what nobody tells you; what most people get wrong; the part everyone misses; the secret is; here's where it gets interesting; this is the part most people skip; few people realize; the real story is.

**Why:** It flatters the writer as the lone insider and delays the claim. A surprising claim doesn't need the setup.

> **Before:** Here's what nobody tells you about hiring: the best candidates aren't looking.
>
> **After:** We found each of our best three hires through a customer referral, and none of them was job hunting at the time.

### 4. One-line closers and kickers (Tier 1)

**Watch for:** a one-sentence paragraph that restates the previous one; That's the real win; That's the whole game; That changes everything; Let that sink in; Read that again; Think about that; Full stop; Period; The lesson? ...; a sentence that names what an example just showed ("This shows the power of consistency"); an aphorism at the end ("Clarity is kindness").

**Why:** It asks the reader to pause on a claim and adds nothing to it. When it repeats after every section, it turns into a verbal tic.

**Fix:** Delete it and end on the last concrete sentence. Don't write a better aphorism to replace it.

> **Before:** We cut our standup from 30 minutes to 10 and nobody missed the extra time.
>
> Less is more.
>
> **After:** We cut our standup from 30 minutes to 10 and nobody missed the extra time.

> **Before:** She answered every support ticket herself for the first year. That's what customer obsession looks like.
>
> **After:** She answered every support ticket herself for the first year, including the 2 a.m. ones from Australia.

### 5. Dramatic fragments (Tier 1)

**Watch for:** stacked fragments ("No meetings. No Slack. Just work."); noun lists as sentences ("Speed. Quality. Cost."); "That's it. That's the post."; "And Y. And Z."; one word per sentence with periods ("Every. Single. Day."); ALL CAPS on one word for drama.

**Why:** Periods between fragments make ordinary content look emphatic.

> **Before:** Then we launched. No ads. No press. No budget. Just a tweet.
>
> **After:** We launched with one tweet and no ad budget. It got 40 signups, and 31 came from a single retweet by a developer we'd never met.

### 6. Negative listing (Tier 1)

**Watch for:** "Not a X. Not a Y. A Z."; "It wasn't X. It wasn't Y. It was Z."

> **Before:** It's not a CRM. It's not a spreadsheet. It's a second brain for your sales team.
>
> **After:** It logs every call and email with a customer in one timeline, so reps stop asking each other "did anyone talk to them?"

### 7. Self-answered questions and rhetorical setups (Tier 1)

**Watch for:** "The result? ..." "Why does this matter? Because..." "What if I told you..." "Think about it." "Sound familiar?" "Plot twist:" "Spoiler:" "The kicker?"

**Why:** The question exists only to be answered in the next breath.

> **Before:** The result? A 40% drop in churn. Why? Because we finally listened.
>
> **After:** Churn dropped 40% in the quarter after we started calling every customer who cancelled.

### 8. Colon reveals (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** a noun phrase, a colon, then a dramatic reveal. "The best part: it's free." "The catch: you need a Mac." "My takeaway: ship sooner."

**Why:** It works like a self-answered question, with a colon in place of the question mark. Use colons for lists, labels and quotes.

> **Before:** The secret ingredient: brown butter.
>
> **After:** The cookies taste nutty because the butter is browned first.

## B. Inflation and borrowed authority

The fact underneath is usually fine. Keep it and strip the dressing.

### 9. Inflated significance (Tier 1)

**Watch for:** marks a pivotal moment; stands as a testament; plays a vital/crucial/key role; underscores the importance; reflects a broader trend; sets the stage for; shaping the future of; an indelible mark; a lasting legacy; a turning point; the evolving landscape; a game-changer; this changes everything; the future looks bright; exciting times ahead; a step in the right direction.

> **Before:** The partnership marks a pivotal moment in our journey and underscores our commitment to accessibility.
>
> **After:** Through the partnership, every course gets human-written captions in Spanish and Portuguese, starting in March.

### 10. "Despite challenges" sections and outlook endings (Tier 1)

**Watch for:** "Despite these challenges, X continues to thrive"; a stock "Challenges and Future Outlook" section; a send-off paragraph about the journey ahead.

> **Before:** Despite facing stiff competition, the brand continues to thrive, poised for an exciting future.
>
> **After:** Two larger competitors launched cheaper versions last year. Sales still grew 12%, mostly from repeat buyers.

### 11. Shallow -ing riders (Tier 1)

**Watch for:** ..., highlighting / underscoring / showcasing / reflecting / emphasizing / symbolizing / ensuring / fostering / contributing to / paving the way for...

**Why:** It's an -ing clause bolted onto a plain fact to make it sound meaningful. It claims an interpretation nobody established.

> **Before:** The app added dark mode, reflecting the team's dedication to user wellbeing.
>
> **After:** The app added dark mode, the most requested feature on the forum for two years running.

### 12. Sales and brochure language (Tier 1)

**Watch for:** nestled; in the heart of; breathtaking; stunning; vibrant; rich (figurative); boasts; hidden gem; must-visit; curated; bespoke; seamless; effortless; world-class; cutting-edge; best-in-class; next-level; unlock; elevate; supercharge; revolutionize; empower; reimagined.

> **Before:** Nestled in the heart of Lisbon, our boutique hotel boasts breathtaking views and a curated selection of local wines.
>
> **After:** The hotel is on a steep street in Alfama. Ask for a room on the fourth floor, where you can see the river from bed. The wine list is all Portuguese, and the bartender will pour a taste of anything before you order.

### 13. Stock AI vocabulary (Tier 1 in clusters, Tier 2 single)

See [words.md](words.md). One "crucial" is fine. "Crucial," "robust," "landscape" and "delve" in the same paragraph are not.

> **Before:** Let's delve into the multifaceted landscape of remote hiring and explore robust strategies for navigating it.
>
> **After:** Remote hiring kept tripping on time zones and on references we couldn't verify. Offers also got stuck in other companies' legal reviews.

### 14. Weasel attribution (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** experts say; studies show; research suggests; many believe; it's widely known; industry reports indicate; according to many; critics argue (unnamed).

**Fix:** Name the source and what it said. If you don't have one, cut the claim or ask the user. Never invent a source.

> **Before:** Studies show that personalized emails dramatically increase engagement.
>
> **After:** When we added the customer's company name to the subject line, open rates went from 22% to 29% over 4,000 sends.

### 15. Prestige lists and vanity metrics (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** "featured in Forbes, TechCrunch, and Inc."; "trusted by thousands"; "an active community of 50,000+"; a list of outlets standing in for what any of them said.

> **Before:** Our founder has been featured in Forbes, Business Insider, and Fast Company.
>
> **After:** Forbes covered our founder's switch from charging per seat to charging per project.

## C. Rhythm and structure by rule

### 16. Forced triads (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** three adjectives, three nouns, three parallel examples, three short sentences followed by a lesson. "Faster, smarter, and more secure." "Innovation, collaboration, and excellence."

**Fix:** Keep the items that are real and distinct. Use two, or four, or one developed properly.

> **Before:** The retreat offers rest, reflection, and renewal.
>
> **After:** The retreat has no wifi and no schedule after 2 p.m. Most people sleep a lot the first day.

> **Before (paragraph scale):** Some customers want speed. Some want price. Some want support. The best companies serve all three.
>
> **After:** Our customers split into two groups: agencies who'll pay double for a same-day turnaround, and solo shops who mostly care about price.

### 17. Metronome rhythm (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** consecutive sentences within a few words of each other in length; every paragraph three sentences long; every paragraph ending on a short punchy line; one sentence per line for a whole post.

**Fix:** Vary length by meaning. Put a long sentence next to a three-word one. Let some paragraphs be one line and others run to six.

> **Before:** We launched in May. Growth was slow at first. Then we changed our pricing. Signups doubled overnight. It was a good lesson.
>
> **After:** We launched in May and spent six weeks watching about four signups a day. In July we dropped the annual-only plan and added monthly billing. Signups doubled that week.

### 18. Template paragraphs and even sections (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** every paragraph built as topic sentence, elaboration, example, wrap-up; five sections of identical length; three pros and three cons; numbered steps padded to a round number.

**Fix:** Real topics are lumpy. Give the hard part room and the easy part one sentence. Sometimes put the point last.

### 19. Filler transitions (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** Moreover; Furthermore; Additionally; In addition; Notably; Importantly; Interestingly; Ultimately; Consequently, opening paragraph after paragraph.

**Fix:** Start with the subject. Save "but" and "so" for real turns.

> **Before:** Additionally, the plan includes priority support. Furthermore, it offers unlimited projects. Moreover, you can cancel anytime.
>
> **After:** The plan includes priority support and unlimited projects, and you can cancel anytime.

### 20. Signposting and meta-commentary (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** In this post, we'll explore; Let's take a look at; As we'll see; As mentioned earlier; Now let's turn to; The rest of this essay explains; It's worth noting that; It's important to remember; Needless to say; It goes without saying; The key takeaway is; This distinction matters; That last part matters more than it sounds.

**Fix:** Delete the announcement and keep the fact after it.

> **Before:** In this article, we'll explore five ways to reduce churn. It's worth noting that every business is different.
>
> **After:** The fastest churn fix we found was a cancellation survey with one required question.

### 21. Summary-recap endings (Tier 1)

**Watch for:** In conclusion; In summary; To sum up; Ultimately; Overall; At the end of the day; a final paragraph that repeats the intro in different words.

**Fix:** End on the last concrete point, a next action, or an open question you actually care about.

### 22. False balance (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** "While some argue X, others believe Y"; "There are pros and cons"; "The truth lies somewhere in between"; "It depends on your needs."

**Fix:** Pick a side and say why, or name the condition: "Use X if you have fewer than ten people; past that, Y."

> **Before:** Both monoliths and microservices have their merits, and the right choice depends on your unique needs.
>
> **After:** Start with a monolith. We split out exactly one service, billing, and only after its deploys started blocking everyone else's.

### 23. Reader sweeps (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** "Whether you're a X or a Y"; "From X to Y" (from startups to Fortune 500s, from beginners to pros); "for everyone from..."

**Fix:** Write to one reader. If range matters, name two real cases.

> **Before:** Whether you're a seasoned chef or a kitchen newbie, this knife will elevate your cooking.
>
> **After:** The blade is short enough for small hands and heavy enough to get through squash without rocking.

### 24. Arguing with no one (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** I'm not saying; Don't get me wrong; To be clear; This isn't to say; Some might argue; You might be thinking; A tempting approach would be.

**Why:** It rebuts an objection that appears nowhere else, usually a leftover from an earlier draft.

> **Before:** Don't get me wrong, I love meetings. I'm not saying we should cancel them all. But most of ours could be emails.
>
> **After:** Most of our meetings could be emails. Last week I counted, and 6 of 9 were status updates.

### 25. Repeated sentence openings (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** three or more sentences in a row starting with the same word ("We... We... We...", "She... She... She..."), when it isn't deliberate rhythm.

> **Before:** We built the prototype in a week. We tested it with five users. We learned that nobody read the tooltips.
>
> **After:** The prototype took a week. Five users tested it, and none of them read a single tooltip.

## D. Voice and agency

### 26. False agency (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** inanimate things doing human verbs: the data tells us; the market rewards; the culture shifted; a decision emerged; the conversation moved toward; the complaint became a fix; the numbers speak for themselves.

**Fix:** Name the human. If no specific person fits, use "you" or "we."

> **Before:** The data told us users wanted simpler onboarding.
>
> **After:** Maya went through 200 session recordings and found most users quit at the third setup screen.

### 27. Passive voice that hides the actor (Tier 2; a single instance is Tier 3)

**Watch for:** mistakes were made; it was decided; it is believed that; the feature was deprecated; improvements have been implemented.

**Keep** passive voice when the actor doesn't matter or is obvious ("The bridge was built in 1932").

> **Before:** It was decided that the launch would be delayed.
>
> **After:** We delayed the launch two weeks because the payment flow failed on Safari.

### 28. Fake-strong verbs (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** serves as; stands as; functions as; acts as; boasts; features; offers (when "has" works); represents; marks; embodies.

> **Before:** The dashboard serves as a centralized hub and boasts real-time analytics.
>
> **After:** The dashboard shows every campaign's spend and signups, updated every five minutes.

### 29. Synonym cycling (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** the same thing renamed each sentence: the app / the platform / the tool / the solution; the author / the writer / the creator.

> **Before:** The app tracks your habits. The platform sends reminders. The tool also shows streaks.
>
> **After:** The app tracks your habits, sends reminders, and shows your streaks.

### 30. Stacked hedges (Tier 2; single hedge is Tier 3)

**Watch for:** could potentially; might possibly; may arguably; it could be said that; in some cases it may; to some extent.

> **Before:** This could potentially help some teams to some extent.
>
> **After:** This helps teams that deploy more than once a day. Below that, you won't notice.

## E. Formatting by rule

### 31. Bold-label bullets (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** every bullet starting with a bold label and a colon, where the label repeats the sentence.

> **Before:**
> - **Speed:** The new version is significantly faster.
> - **Security:** Security has been enhanced.
> - **Design:** The design has been refreshed.
>
> **After:** Pages load in under a second now, down from three. We also added two-factor login and moved the settings menu to the top bar.

### 32. Decoration (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** emoji bullets (🚀 💡 ✅ 🎯); arrows (→) as bullets; random bold mid-paragraph; fake-bold Unicode (𝗹𝗶𝗸𝗲 𝘁𝗵𝗶𝘀); horizontal rules between every section; Title Case On Every Heading.

**Fix:** Plain hyphen bullets or prose. Sentence-case headings. Bold one thing per screen at most, or nothing.

### 33. Headings and bullets the content doesn't need (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** headings over two-sentence sections; a heading followed by a sentence that restates it; bullets for what is really a connected argument; a two-row table.

> **Before:**
> ## Why It Matters
> This matters because it affects everyone.
>
> **After:** (Delete the heading and its restating sentence. Start with the content.)

### 34. Curly/straight quote mixing (Tier 3)

Mixed curly and straight quotes in one text suggest pasting. Normalize to whatever the destination uses.

### 35. Hyphenated pairs after the noun (Tier 3)

"A long-term plan" keeps the hyphen. "The plan is long term" drops it. Words that are always hyphenated (third-party, self-serve) keep it everywhere.

## F. Leftovers

Remove outright.

### 36. Chatbot residue (Tier 1)

**Watch for:** Great question; Certainly!; Absolutely!; Of course!; I'd be happy to; Here's a draft; I hope this helps; Let me know if you'd like me to; Feel free to; Would you like me to; You're absolutely right.

> **Before:** Great question! Here's a quick overview. The festival runs June 3–5 in Austin. I hope this helps!
>
> **After:** The festival runs June 3–5 in Austin.

### 37. Knowledge-limit disclaimers and guesses (Tier 1)

**Watch for:** as of my last update; while specific details are limited; based on available information; it appears that; likely grew up; it is believed; not widely documented.

**Fix:** State what's known. Cut the guess. If something is missing, ask the user.

### 38. Leaked markup and placeholders (Tier 1)

**Watch for:** `oaicite`, `contentReference`, `turn0search0`, `utm_source=chatgpt.com`, `[cite: 1]`, 【】 brackets, †; "[Your Name]", "[Company]", "Insert statistic here", "Lorem ipsum."

### 39. Writing about the document instead of the subject (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** "This post was written to..."; "The table below compares..."; "This section covers..."; "I've compiled the following..."

**Fix:** Cut it, since the reader can see the table.

## G. Writing for the wrong reader

### 40. Re-explaining what the reader already knows (Tier 2)

A model writes for a reader with zero context. In a reply, comment, or follow-up email, the reader already has the background. Rebuilding it buries the answer.

**Fix:** Lead with the decision or answer. Keep only the reasoning that would change whether the reader agrees.

> **Before:** Thanks for flagging the pricing page issue. As you mentioned, the annual toggle shows the monthly price, which could confuse customers. I looked into it and found the cause was a caching problem in the CDN. I've now cleared the cache and added a rule. It should be fixed.
>
> **After:** Fixed. The CDN was serving a stale copy, so I cleared it and added a rule so that page never caches for more than five minutes.

### 41. Restating the question (Tier 2)

**Watch for:** "You asked about X. X is an important topic..." "When it comes to pricing your course, there are several factors..."

**Fix:** Answer in the first sentence.

> **Before:** When it comes to choosing a newsletter platform, there are many factors to consider.
>
> **After:** Use Buttondown if you want Markdown and no tracking pixels. Use Beehiiv if you plan to sell ads.
