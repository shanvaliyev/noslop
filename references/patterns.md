# Pattern catalog

Each entry lists what to watch for, the fix, and a before/after pair. One instance of most patterns is ordinary human writing, so act when they cluster or when a sentence gives the reader nothing new. Chatbot residue, leftovers and dashes are the exceptions: remove them every time.

Social posts get slack on hooks, fragments, closers and questions to readers; see [formats.md](formats.md).

The "After" examples use specific details to show what a fixed sentence looks like. In real work those details must come from the user or the source. If you don't have them, ask, or write the plainer sentence. Never invent a detail to make a rewrite land.

## Staging instead of stating

### 1. Not X but Y

**Watch for:** it's not X, it's Y; not just X but Y; more than just a; the contrast split across two sentences ("This doesn't mean X. It means Y."); a clipped negative tail ("..., no guesswork").

The negative half rebuts something nobody claimed, which makes the positive half sound bigger without adding information. Keep it when the reader really believes X, or when both halves carry facts.

> **Before:** Great onboarding isn't about features. It's about momentum.
>
> **After:** Get new users to their first finished report inside ten minutes, and schedule the rest of onboarding after that.

> **Keep (real correction):** Everyone assumed the outage was the database. It wasn't. A DNS record had expired.

### 2. Staged openers and faux-insight setups

**Watch for:** Here's the thing; Let's dive in; Let me be clear; The truth is; Honestly?; Real talk; What nobody tells you; The part everyone misses; Here's where it gets interesting.

The writer announces the point, or stages a moment of candor, instead of making the point. "Honestly" or "look" inside an ordinary casual sentence is fine, and so is "I'll be honest" before a real admission.

> **Before:** Here's what nobody tells you about hiring: the best candidates aren't looking.
>
> **After:** We found our best three hires through customer referrals, and none of them was job hunting at the time.

### 3. Closers, kickers and recap endings

**Watch for:** a one-sentence paragraph restating the one before; Let that sink in; That's the real win; That changes everything; a sentence naming what an example just showed; an aphorism at the end; In conclusion; Ultimately; a final paragraph that repeats the intro; "the future looks bright" send-offs.

Delete it and end on the last concrete sentence, a next step, or a question you actually care about. Don't write a better aphorism to replace it.

> **Before:** She answered every support ticket herself for the first year. That's what customer obsession looks like.
>
> **After:** She answered every support ticket herself for the first year, including the 2 a.m. ones from Australia.

### 4. Dramatic fragments and negative listing

**Watch for:** "No meetings. No Slack. Just work."; "Speed. Quality. Cost."; "Every. Single. Day."; "Not a CRM. Not a spreadsheet. A second brain."

Periods between fragments make ordinary content look emphatic. Concrete fragments in a story are milder; merging them into one sentence is usually enough.

> **Before:** Then we launched. No ads. No press. No budget. Just a tweet.
>
> **After:** We launched with one tweet and no ad budget. It got 40 signups, and 31 came from a single retweet.

### 5. Self-answered questions and colon reveals

**Watch for:** "The result? ..."; "Why does this matter? Because..."; "Sound familiar?"; "The best part: it's free."; "The catch: you need a Mac."

The question or colon exists only to set up the next few words. Make the statement.

> **Before:** The result? A 40% drop in churn. Why? Because we finally listened.
>
> **After:** Churn dropped 40% in the quarter after we started calling every customer who cancelled.

## Inflation and borrowed authority

The fact underneath is usually fine. Keep it and strip the dressing.

### 6. Inflated significance and -ing riders

**Watch for:** marks a pivotal moment; stands as a testament; plays a vital role; sets the stage for; a lasting legacy; "despite challenges, X continues to thrive"; a plain fact with an -ing clause bolted on (..., highlighting / showcasing / reflecting / fostering...).

> **Before:** The app added dark mode, reflecting the team's dedication to user wellbeing.
>
> **After:** The app added dark mode, the most requested feature on the forum for two years running.

### 7. Sales and brochure language

**Watch for:** nestled; in the heart of; breathtaking; vibrant; hidden gem; curated; seamless; effortless; world-class; cutting-edge; unlock; elevate; supercharge; revolutionize; empower.

Say what the thing is, what it does and what it costs.

> **Before:** Nestled in the heart of Lisbon, our boutique hotel boasts breathtaking views.
>
> **After:** The hotel is on a steep street in Alfama. From the fourth floor you can see the river from bed.

### 8. Stock AI vocabulary

See [words.md](words.md). One "crucial" is fine. "Crucial," "robust," "landscape" and "delve" in one paragraph are not, and swapping each for a synonym won't fix it. Rewrite the paragraph around what it's trying to say.

### 9. Borrowed authority and vanity proof

**Watch for:** experts say; studies show; research suggests; many believe; "featured in Forbes, TechCrunch and Inc."; "trusted by thousands."

Name the source and what it said, or the customer and their result. If the user has none, cut the claim or ask. Never invent one.

> **Before:** Studies show that personalized emails dramatically increase engagement.
>
> **After:** When we added the customer's company name to the subject line, open rates went from 22% to 29% over 4,000 sends.

## Rhythm and structure by rule

### 10. Forced triads

**Watch for:** three adjectives or nouns that exist for rhythm ("fast, flexible, and future-proof"); three short sentences followed by a lesson.

Keep three when there are three real things. Otherwise use two, or four, or develop one properly. If a triad holds three real claims, break the rhythm instead of dropping one.

> **Before:** The retreat offers rest, reflection, and renewal.
>
> **After:** The retreat has no wifi and no schedule after 2 p.m. Most people sleep a lot the first day.

### 11. Metronome rhythm and template paragraphs

**Watch for:** consecutive sentences of matching length; every paragraph three sentences long, built as claim, elaboration, example, wrap-up; every section the same length; three sentences in a row opening with "We."

Vary length by meaning. Real topics are lumpy: the hard part gets room and the easy part gets a sentence.

> **Before:** We launched in May. Growth was slow at first. Then we changed our pricing. Signups doubled overnight. It was a good lesson.
>
> **After:** We launched in May and spent six weeks watching about four signups a day. In July we added monthly billing, and signups doubled that week.

### 12. Filler transitions and signposting

**Watch for:** Moreover, Furthermore, Additionally or Notably opening paragraph after paragraph; In this post we'll explore; As we'll see; It's worth noting that; The key takeaway is; This distinction matters; "The table below compares..."

Delete the announcement and keep the fact after it. Save "but" and "so" for real turns.

> **Before:** In this article, we'll explore five ways to reduce churn. It's worth noting that every business is different.
>
> **After:** The fastest churn fix we found was a cancellation survey with one required question.

### 13. False balance and reader sweeps

**Watch for:** "Some argue X, others Y"; "The truth lies somewhere in between"; "It depends on your needs"; "Whether you're a founder or a freelancer"; "From startups to enterprises."

Pick a side, or name the condition under which each answer is right. Write to one reader; if range matters, name two real cases.

> **Before:** Both monoliths and microservices have their merits, and the right choice depends on your unique needs.
>
> **After:** Start with a monolith. We split out one service, billing, and only after its deploys started blocking everyone else's.

### 14. Stacked hedges and arguing with no one

**Watch for:** could potentially; might possibly; may arguably; to some extent; I'm not saying; Don't get me wrong; To be clear; You might be thinking.

Use one qualifier, and only when the doubt is real. Cut rebuttals of objections nobody raised.

> **Before:** Don't get me wrong, I love meetings. But this could potentially be an email.
>
> **After:** Six of last week's nine meetings were status updates that could have been emails.

## Voice and agency

### 15. Hidden actors and fake-strong verbs

**Watch for:** the data tells us; the market rewards; a decision emerged; mistakes were made; it was decided; serves as; stands as; boasts; represents.

Name who acted, or use "we" or "you." Use is, has, does. Passive voice is fine when the actor doesn't matter ("The bridge was built in 1932").

> **Before:** It was decided that the launch would be delayed.
>
> **After:** We delayed the launch two weeks because the payment flow failed on Safari.

### 16. Synonym cycling

**Watch for:** the same thing renamed each sentence: the app, the platform, the tool, the solution. Pick one name and keep it.

## Formatting by rule

### 17. Formatting the content doesn't need

**Watch for:** a bold label on every bullet that repeats the sentence after it; emoji or arrow bullets; Title Case Headings; headings over two-sentence sections; a heading followed by a sentence restating it; bullets for a connected argument; random bold mid-paragraph; fake-bold Unicode.

Use prose or plain bullets and sentence-case headings. Bold labels are fine when each label is short and the sentence after it adds facts.

> **Before:**
> - **Speed:** The new version is significantly faster.
> - **Security:** Security has been enhanced.
>
> **After:** Pages load in under a second now, down from three, and we added two-factor login.

## Leftovers

### 18. Chatbot residue, disclaimers and markup

**Watch for:** Great question; Certainly!; I'd be happy to; Here's a draft; I hope this helps; Let me know if you'd like; as of my last update; based on available information; `oaicite`, `turn0search0`, `[cite: 1]`, `utm_source=chatgpt.com`; "[Your Name]", "Insert statistic here."

Delete them. If a disclaimer covers a real gap, ask the user instead of guessing.

### 19. Em and en dashes

**Watch for:** "The update — finally — ships today"; " -- " used as a dash.

Use a comma, period, colon or parentheses. Leave dashes in code, URLs and number ranges, and keep them when the user's voice sample uses them.

## Writing for the wrong reader

### 20. Re-explaining and restating the question

**Watch for:** a reply that rebuilds context the reader already has; "When it comes to choosing X, there are many factors to consider."

Lead with the decision or answer. Keep only the reasoning that would change whether the reader agrees.

> **Before:** Thanks for flagging the pricing page issue. As you mentioned, the annual toggle shows the monthly price, which could confuse customers. I looked into it and found a caching problem in the CDN. It should be fixed now.
>
> **After:** Fixed. The CDN was serving a stale copy, so I cleared it and capped that page's cache at five minutes.
